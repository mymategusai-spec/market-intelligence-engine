## 2. Read this correctly, or it will mislead you

The naive reading — "Nagano is 39.6% occupied, therefore ski lodges are a bad business" — is
**wrong**, and it is important to say why before using these numbers.

**These are annual figures.** A winter-dominant property that trades hard from late December to
early April and closes the rest of the year is **structurally capped at roughly 25–33% annual
occupancy even at 100% winter occupancy**. Nagano's 39.6% is therefore partly an artefact of
seasonality, not a measure of failure.

The correct model is:

```
annual revenue = winter season length (nights) × winter occupancy × ADR
                 + shoulder/summer contribution
```

**not** `365 × annual occupancy × ADR`. Any model using annual occupancy for a winter-dominant
asset is wrong by construction.

`CALCULATION` — cross-check: Hakuba's foreign overnight stays are 84.6% Dec–Mar. If a property's
demand follows that shape, a 30% annual occupancy implies roughly **75% occupancy across the
four winter months**. That is a healthy winter business and a poor annual number simultaneously.
Both descriptions are true.

## 3. What the numbers *do* legitimately establish

### a) Hokkaido genuinely trades better, and it is not seasonality
Hokkaido's 旅館 occupancy is **43.5%, rank 6 nationally** — against Nagano 27.9% (rank 41) and
Niigata 26.6% (rank 44). Hokkaido has snow seasonality too, so the gap is **not** explained by
winter dominance. It reflects real four-season demand (lavender, Furano summer, Sapporo) plus a
longer, more reliable season.

**This materially strengthens the Hokkaido case** and is independent of the land-price and snow
evidence already gathered. Three separate lines of evidence now point the same way.

### b) Nagano and Niigata carry legacy accommodation oversupply
Nagano's total overnight stays grew **+11.9% in 2024** and are **+11.4% against 2019** — demand is
genuinely rising. Yet occupancy is last in Japan.

Rising demand plus lowest-in-country occupancy means one thing: **there are too many rooms.**
Nagano and Niigata are carrying bubble-era and post-1998-Olympics accommodation stock that
current demand still does not fill.

This is the **same mechanism as Yuzawa**, operating at prefecture scale — and it reframes the
forward-supply question. The brief asks whether *new* supply threatens occupancy. In Nagano and
Niigata the more pressing fact is that **the oversupply already exists**. New competing capacity
lands on a market that is not full to begin with.

### c) 簡易宿所 occupancy is the number that should worry a small-lodge buyer
Simple lodging — the licence class most likely to fit a small owner-operated lodge — runs
**15.3% in Nagano and 11.5% in Niigata**, against 25.4% in Hokkaido and 29.0% nationally.

Even after discounting for seasonality, Nagano and Niigata simple lodgings run at roughly **half**
the national rate and well under Hokkaido. Some of that is part-time and hobby operators dragging
the average down. But a buyer modelling a small lodge in Nagano at 60% annual occupancy is
assuming performance **four times** the prefecture average for that facility class.

### d) National demand is no longer uniformly rising
`FACT` — 2025 national overnight stays: total 653.5m (**−0.8%**), Japanese 475.6m (**−3.8%**),
foreign 177.9m (**+8.2%**). *(Figures via search index of the JTA 2025 release; the PDF returned
404 on direct fetch and this is recorded as a blocker.)*

**Foreign growth did not offset domestic decline in 2025.** Every bullish argument in this project
so far has rested on inbound growth. Domestic demand is the larger share of Japanese ski
accommodation, and it is shrinking.

## 4. ADR — still missing, and it is the biggest remaining gap

No reliable ADR figures were obtained. What exists is weak:

- `OPINION` — a booking-platform aggregator showed Hakuba chalets around **US$673–794/night**,
  described as December and early-January dates. Whole-chalet, unknown capacity, unknown
  inclusions, single source. **Not usable for modelling.**
- `FACT` — ryokan ADR is commonly 2–4× business-hotel ADR because rates typically include meals.
  Relevant structurally: a self-catering lodge and a meals-inclusive ryokan are **not**
  rate-comparable, and any benchmark must state which model it describes.

The brief asks for evidence-backed ADR ranges by market and season. **On current evidence I
cannot produce them honestly.** One aggregator quote for one market is not a range, and inventing
peak/shoulder/summer ADR for six markets would be exactly the false precision the brief forbids.

## 5. What this changes

1. **The revenue model must be seasonal, not annual.** Annual occupancy is the wrong denominator
   for a winter asset, and any source quoting it as a lodge benchmark is misleading.
2. **Hokkaido's case strengthens on a third independent axis** — occupancy, after snow regime and
   land-price stability.
3. **Nagano and Niigata have an existing oversupply problem**, not merely a future one. Forward
   supply must be assessed against a market that is already not full.
4. **A new risk enters the register: domestic demand decline** (−3.8% in 2025). Inbound growth is
   real but is not currently offsetting it nationally.
5. **The `simple_lodging` occupancy gap between Hokkaido and Nagano/Niigata (25.4% vs 11.5–15.3%)
   is now one of the sharpest quantitative discriminators between the candidate regions.**
