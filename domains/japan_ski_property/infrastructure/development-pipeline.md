## 2. The finding: Niseko's forward supply is ~16× Myoko's

`CALCULATION` — Niseko has roughly **774 weighted committed rooms** against Myoko's **~47**.
Hotel101 alone (482 rooms, structural phase complete) is **more than ten times** Myoko's entire
committed pipeline.

**This inverts the simple reading of the region comparison.** Hokkaido has looked stronger on
snow regime, land-price stability and occupancy. But it is also absorbing by far the largest
increase in competing capacity — and it is doing so into a prefecture already running 59.8%
occupancy, with resort hotels at 50.5%.

`HYPOTHESIS` — Niseko is closer to the top of its supply cycle than to the bottom. 774 rooms
arriving into a market that is roughly half empty on an annual basis is a genuine risk to
achievable rates and occupancy for a small independent lodge competing for the same guests.

This is testable and should be tested: it needs existing room counts per town, which are the
single biggest gap in this phase.

## 3. Myoko: the pipeline is small, luxury, and a double-edged sword

Six Senses (57 rooms) and an MGallery refurbishment (38) are **not volume supply**. They are
**brand and positioning** plays.

Two opposite effects, and the evidence does not yet separate them:

**Bull reading.** A Six Senses and an Accor MGallery validate Myoko internationally, draw
higher-spending guests, lift the whole market's rate ceiling, and improve the town's services.
A modest lodge nearby benefits from spillover it could never generate alone.

**Bear reading.** They reposition Myoko upmarket. Guests arriving for a Six Senses do not
overflow into an unrenovated pension. The visible effect is to raise the standard guests expect,
raising the renovation bar for every existing operator — while the new supply captures the
premium end. Land prices rise on anticipation, so entry cost rises, while the modest-lodge
segment gains little.

**The bear reading is currently better supported**, for one specific reason: Myoko's land prices
have *not* risen despite the PCG programme being announced, funded and permitted. If the market
believed the spillover thesis, land would already be repricing. It is not.

`OPINION` — I would not treat the Six Senses as bullish for a sub-A$1m lodge in Myoko without
evidence of rate uplift in the *existing* accommodation stock. That evidence does not exist yet
and is a defined next step.

## 4. Status discipline applied

Two entries were deliberately downgraded from what the sources implied:

- **Six Senses** is recorded as `approved`, not `under_construction`. The source says permitting
  was expected March 2026 and construction "commencing April 2026". It is now August 2026 and
  **no independent confirmation of site works was found.** Announced start dates slip routinely;
  upgrading the status requires evidence, not the passage of time.
- **The "22 developments by 2029" figure** is recorded at `announced` with no room count, and
  flagged. It comes from an agency with a transactional interest, and an aggregate project count
  from an interested party is the weakest form of pipeline evidence.

**No unweighted room total appears anywhere in this analysis.**

## 5. The gap that blocks the forward supply ratio

The brief asks for **tourism demand growth versus future accommodation supply growth**. That
ratio cannot be computed yet, because **existing room supply per town is unknown**.

Without the denominator, 774 new rooms in Niseko could be a 5% increase or a 50% increase. The
difference is the entire question.

`CALCULATION` — a rough sense of scale: Hokkaido recorded 44.6m overnight stays in 2024 across the
whole prefecture, but Niseko-area supply is a small fraction of that. Until the town-level room
count exists, the pipeline is a list, not an assessment.

**This is the highest-priority Phase 11 task.**
