# Myoko: bull case, bear case, verdict

**Phase:** 12 (dedicated deep dive) · **Status:** Evidence-complete enough for a provisional call
**Last updated:** 2026-08-20

> The question: **is Myoko genuinely an early-stage opportunity, or is the market narrative
> running ahead of economic fundamentals?**

---

## 1. The contradiction, stated precisely

| Evidence | Direction |
| --- | --- |
| ¥35bn raised of up to ¥70bn; Six Senses (57 rooms) permitted; MGallery (38) in planning; masterplan to 2034 | **Bull** |
| Foreign overnight stays 59,721 (2018) → 120,000 (FY2023), roughly doubling | **Bull** |
| Australians ~70% of inbound, staying **several weeks to two months**, FIT not package | **Bull** |
| Walkable onsen village with 50+ restaurants and bars, lifts adjacent | **Bull** |
| Land prices **−0.79%** in 2026; bottomed 2022; **+5.3% in four years**; still 63% below 1993 peak | **Bear** |
| Population projected **−46.7% by 2050** | **Bear** |
| Takada, the nearest long-record station, shows **marked long-term snowfall decline** | **Bear** |
| Niigata simple-lodging occupancy **11.5%**, rank 42; ryokan 26.6%, rank 44 | **Bear** |
| Myoko is only ~23% of Niigata's foreign stays; prefecture +55% is **not** Myoko's | **Bear** |
| Myoko's own plan targets ~7%/yr foreign growth to 2029 | **Bear** |
| Three observed listing price reductions, two of them in Myoko (−27.4%, −15.0%) | **Bear** |

---

## 2. The bull case, stated at its strongest

1. **Capital is committed, not rumoured.** ¥35bn raised, a Six Senses permitted and an Accor
   MGallery in planning. This is not a press release; it is money.
2. **Demand doubled in five years.** 59,721 → 120,000 foreign overnight stays. That is real,
   measured growth from municipal and MLIT sources.
3. **The demand is unusually high quality.** Weeks-to-months FIT stays are the best possible
   profile for a self-contained lodge: fewer turnovers, lower cleaning cost per night, less
   marketing dependence, higher effective occupancy per booking.
4. **The product is genuinely good.** A walkable onsen village with 50+ venues beside the lifts,
   with the "old Japan" character international visitors say they want — and which Niseko and
   Hakuba have partly lost.
5. **Entry price is the lowest of any credible market.** ¥19,904/m², roughly one-sixth of Kutchan.
6. **Land prices lag demand.** Visitors arrive before capital does; land reprices only when buyers
   compete. On this reading Myoko in 2026 is Hakuba in roughly 2016.
7. **The Australians are already there.** ~70% of inbound. These owners understand that customer.

The bull case is coherent and rests on real evidence. It is not a fantasy.

---

## 3. The bear case, stated at its strongest

1. **Eight years of announced investment has not moved land prices.** This is the single most
   damaging fact. PCG has been visible in Myoko for years; the money is raised, the hotel is
   permitted — and 2026 land prices still fell 0.79%. **If the spillover thesis were credible to
   people with local knowledge and capital, land would already be repricing.** It is not.
2. **The tourism argument was overstated and has been corrected.** Niigata's +55% is largely not
   Myoko. Myoko is ~23% of the prefecture and grew at roughly half Hakuba's rate over the same
   period. The headline number that made Myoko look like the fastest-growing market was a
   prefecture aggregate containing Yuzawa and Niigata City.
3. **Occupancy is among the worst in Japan.** Niigata simple lodging 11.5% (rank 42), ryokan
   26.6% (rank 44). Even discounting heavily for winter seasonality, this is roughly half
   Hokkaido's equivalent. **The market a lodge would actually compete in is not full.**
4. **Single-market concentration.** ~70% Australian, against Hakuba's 44.7%. This is correlated
   with the owners' own AUD/JPY exposure: a weaker AUD would cut their returns *and* their guests'
   willingness to travel, simultaneously.
5. **Demographics are severe.** −46.7% by 2050 undermines labour, services and exit demand across
   the entire hold period.
6. **Snow risk is real and local.** Low-elevation Japan Sea side; the nearest long-record station
   shows marked decline.
7. **The narrative is largely produced by interested parties.** The developer's affiliated
   brokerage publishes the land-price commentary promoting the market it is developing.
8. **Observed prices are falling.** Two of three observed reductions are Myoko properties.
9. **The luxury pipeline may not help a modest lodge.** Six Senses guests do not overflow into an
   unrenovated pension. New luxury supply raises the standard guests expect — raising the
   renovation bar for existing operators while capturing the premium itself.

---

## 4. Testing the bull case's central claim

The bull case reduces to: **land lags demand, so Myoko now resembles Hakuba earlier.**

That is a testable claim, and Hakuba's own history tests it.

`FACT` — Hakuba's foreign overnight stays: 2014 **77,724** → 2018 **164,377**. Hakuba's land
prices bottomed in **2018** at ¥8,686/m² and rose from 2019.

So in Hakuba, land turned **within roughly one to four years** of demand doubling — not eight.

Myoko's demand doubled between 2018 and FY2023. Under the Hakuba pattern, land should have turned
by roughly 2024–2025. **Instead 2026 land prices fell.**

`CALCULATION`, medium confidence — **the lag observed in Myoko is already longer than the lag
Hakuba exhibited.** The "land simply lags" explanation is therefore weaker than it first appears.
Something else is holding Myoko's land values down.

Candidate explanations, none yet established:
- Demand growth is real but too small in absolute terms (120k stays) to move a whole municipality.
- Growth is concentrated in Akakura while the municipal average is dominated by the Arai-side town
  — consistent with the observed 14× within-municipality spread, and with the cheap portal stock
  sitting in the town centre rather than the ski villages.
- Depopulation is a persistent drag that tourism has not offset.

**The second explanation is the most interesting and is directly testable**: if Akakura land is
rising while Myoko's municipal average falls, the bull case survives in the sub-market that
actually matters. **This has not been checked, and it is the single most valuable outstanding
test in the whole Myoko question.**

---

## 5. Verdict

`HYPOTHESIS`, medium confidence:

> **The narrative is running ahead of the fundamentals at the municipal level — but the question
> is not yet settled at the neighbourhood level, and that is where it should be decided.**

More precisely:

- **As a land-appreciation play, Myoko is not currently supported by evidence.** Eight years of
  committed capital have not moved municipal land prices, and the tourism argument that appeared
  to support it was a prefecture aggregate that does not survive disaggregation.
- **As an operating business, Myoko is the most attractive market examined.** Walkable onsen
  village, weeks-to-months FIT stays, 50+ venues, lowest entry price, and an Australian guest base
  these owners understand.

**These are different investments.** The brief asks about a property used personally, operated
commercially, *and* benefiting from appreciation. Myoko currently supports the middle claim
strongly, the first well, and the third poorly.

**Recommended posture: `WATCH`, with a defined trigger** — not `NO`.

### Triggers that would upgrade Myoko

1. **Akakura-specific land prices rise** while the municipal average stays flat. *(Most important
   — tests the sub-market hypothesis directly.)*
2. Municipal land prices turn positive in the 2027 or 2028 publication.
3. Six Senses construction is **confirmed started** on site.
4. Myoko-specific occupancy or ADR shows rate growth in existing stock.
5. A licensed operating property is acquirable at a total project cost supported by a modelled NOI.

### Triggers that would downgrade Myoko to `NO`

1. Land prices continue falling through 2028 with the Six Senses complete or near-complete.
2. Australian share rises further while total growth stalls — concentration without growth.
3. Sprinkler or fire-compliance costs for a lodge of viable capacity prove prohibitive.
4. Akakura land prices are found to be falling *with* the municipal average — killing the
   sub-market explanation.

---

## 6. What this changes

- The Myoko case is now **specific rather than atmospheric**: it is an operating-business case,
  not an appreciation case, and it should be argued and modelled that way.
- **The next Myoko research task is narrow and high-value**: get Akakura-level land prices. One
  data point could move this from `WATCH` to either direction.
- Nothing here says Myoko is a bad place to own a lodge. It says the *appreciation* story that
  makes it look like a bargain is, on current evidence, not visible in the data.
