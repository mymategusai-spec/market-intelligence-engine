# Guest capacity: what determines the sweet spot

**Phase:** 26 · **Status:** Constraints identified. Optimum NOT determinable yet, and this
document explains precisely why.
**Last updated:** 2026-08-20

> The brief's instruction: *the objective is not to declare a winner yet — it is to determine what
> data is needed to identify the likely sweet spot.* That is what this does.

---

## 1. Four constraints, and only one of them is bedrooms

### a) Bathrooms bind before bedrooms — observed, not theorised
From the 51 candidate properties collected, bathroom counts are consistently the limiting factor:

| Property | Beds | Baths | Ratio |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Myoko Forest Lodge | 8 (sleeps 20) | 2 + 6 WC/basin | 1:10 guests |
| Wonderland Chalet (A$872k) | multiple | **1** | severe |
| Alpen View Art Villa | 6 (sleeps 16) | 2 | 1:8 |
| **Ski-in Ski-out Chalet Tsugaike** | 8 | **6** | **1:1.3 beds** |
| **One Happo** | 5 | **6** | better than 1:1 |
| **Yotei View Retreat** | 3 | **3 ensuite** | 1:1 |

`FACT` — Most Japanese ski lodges were built as *pensions* for domestic groups who accepted shared
bathing (the onsen culture makes this normal). Western guests paying premium rates increasingly do
not. **Adding bathrooms is plumbing work in a snow-country building — among the most expensive
renovation categories.**

**Consequence:** capacity is not free to scale. Going from 12 to 20 guests may require 2–3
additional bathrooms, and in an older building that can dominate a renovation budget.

### b) The sprinkler threshold — the binding regulatory constraint, still unresolved
`FACT` — For 簡易宿所, fire-compliance cost for a **detached house** is ~¥300,000. For larger
buildings it ranges to **¥10,000,000**, and the swing factor is whether **sprinklers** become
mandatory, which depends on **floor area, number of storeys and occupant capacity**.

**This is the single most important unknown for the capacity question.** Somewhere between 6 and
20+ guests there is a threshold at which the compliance cost steps up by an order of magnitude.
A property at 15 guests might be viable and the same property at 22 might not.

**Nothing else about capacity can be optimised until this threshold is known**, because it
plausibly dominates every other consideration.

### c) Cleaning and turnover cost scales with *bookings*, not guests
Cleaning cost is driven by changeovers. A 12-guest lodge let to one group for 7 nights is **one**
changeover; the same lodge let room-by-room for 2-night stays is many.

This is where the Myoko tourism finding becomes economically material. `FACT` — Australian FIT
visitors to Myoko stay **several weeks to two months**. At those durations turnover cost per
occupied night collapses, and a larger property becomes far more manageable remotely.

**Longer stays make higher capacity more viable**, and this varies by market. Capacity cannot be
optimised independently of the demand profile of the specific town.

### d) Staffing crosses a threshold the owners cannot cross remotely
Below roughly 10–12 guests, a property can plausibly run on a cleaner plus a remote booking
manager. Above that — and certainly if meals are served — it starts to need on-site presence.

`FACT` — minpaku registration legally requires a **management contact reachable 24/7**. Owners in
Australia cannot be that contact. Larger capacity increases the frequency of incidents that
require someone local.

**`ASSUMPTIONS.md` C2 (management procurable) tightens as capacity rises**, and it is already a
low-confidence gating assumption.

## 2. What can be said now

| Capacity | Assessment |
| --- | --- |
| **6** | Under-uses fixed costs. Licensing, compliance, furnishing and management barely scale down — the same problem the capital model found at the property level. Likely too small. |
| **8–10** | Plausible floor. One cleaner, remote management, probably below sprinkler thresholds, bathroom ratio achievable. |
| **12–16** | **Where the candidate stock actually sits.** Most collected lodges are 8–11 bedrooms sleeping 16–20. Bathroom ratio becomes the binding constraint. |
| **20+** | Revenue potential highest, but crosses into likely sprinkler liability, needs on-site staff, and stresses the remote-management assumption hardest. |

`OPINION`, low confidence — the sweet spot is **probably 10–16**, because it is where the
available stock is, where bathroom ratios remain fixable, and where remote management stays
plausible. **This is a hypothesis shaped by supply and constraints, not a conclusion from
modelled returns** — and it cannot be one until ADR exists.

## 3. What data would settle it

In priority order:

1. **The sprinkler threshold** — floor area, storeys and occupant capacity at which sprinklers
   become mandatory for 簡易宿所 and 旅館. **Single highest-value unknown in this phase.** Source:
   local fire authority, per municipality.
2. **ADR by capacity band** — does a 16-guest lodge earn 2× a 8-guest lodge, or 1.4×? Nightly rate
   does not scale linearly with beds, and without this no revenue comparison is possible.
3. **Cleaning cost per changeover** by market, plus typical length of stay per market.
4. **Bathroom addition cost** in a snow-country building — the marginal cost of capacity.
5. **The management-capacity threshold**: at what guest count do local managers require on-site
   staff, and what does that cost? From Phase 16 providers.

**Only item 1 is a hard constraint. Items 2–5 are optimisation.** Getting the threshold wrong
could invalidate a purchase; getting the optimum slightly wrong merely costs some return.

---

## 4. Why no revenue model appears here

The brief asks for revenue potential by capacity. Producing it would require ADR, and no reliable
ADR was obtained (see [accommodation market evidence](../tourism/accommodation-market-evidence.md)).

Modelling revenue for six capacity bands across five markets from a single unverified aggregator
quote would generate 30 numbers, none of which would mean anything. **The constraints above are
real findings; invented revenue would not be.**
