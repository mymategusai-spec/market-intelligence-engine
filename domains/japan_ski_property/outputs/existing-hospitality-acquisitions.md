## 2. The vendor yield claims do not survive a bottom-up model

**This is the most important finding in this document.**

Echo Hotel Hakuba is marketed at *"circa 10% net"*. Ezomurasaki at *"~8% p.a."*.

The bottom-up models built in this project produce a **maximum of 3.17% on total project cost**
across every property and every scenario — and that best case assumes 70% high-season occupancy.

`CALCULATION` — testing the Echo Hotel claim: 10% net on ¥695m implies **¥69.5m (A$618k) of net
income annually.** At a 25–30% net margin that requires roughly **¥230–280m of gross revenue** from
13 rooms. Across a 76-night high season that is over ¥230,000 per room per night at full
occupancy — implausible — so the claim must rest on year-round trading at rates far above anything
evidenced, or on a different definition of "net".

**Three possible explanations, and the buyer must establish which:**

1. **Different denominator.** Vendor yields are typically quoted on *purchase price*, not total
   project cost, and often exclude acquisition costs, capex reserve and owner's management time.
2. **Different numerator.** "Net" may mean net of direct costs only — before management, platform
   commission, capex reserve, tax and vacancy. Those are ~40% of gross in this project's models.
3. **The claim is simply unsupported marketing.**

`OPINION`, high confidence — **no vendor yield claim should be relied on without audited accounts.**
The gap between marketed yields (8–10%) and modelled yields (1–3%) is roughly **3×**, and it is
too large to be a modelling quibble. Either the models are materially too conservative — in which
case the whole project's economics improve — or the claims are inflated.

**Resolving this is now one of the highest-value tasks in the project**, because it moves the
central financial conclusion in either direction. It requires **audited financial statements**
(決算書) for at least one operating business.

## 3. Ranking

Ranked on strategic fit for two Australians operating remotely, **not** on vendor yield claims.

### Tier 1 — genuinely worth investigating

**1. Myoko Ski Out Onsen ryokan — ¥150m / A$1.33m — Akakura, Myoko**
The best fit found anywhere in this project. It is an **operating ryokan** (so licensed and
compliant), in **Akakura Onsen** — now the only submarket with measured land appreciation
(+7.07%) — steps from the village with a short walk to the lift, with onsen baths. It sits in the
market with the **highest measured snowfall** of any candidate (1,061 cm) and the **lowest forward
supply pressure**.
*Unknowns:* guest capacity, room count, building age, condition, revenue. All are answerable.

**2. Niseko Freeride Lodge — ¥395m / A$3.52m — ~40 guests, 10+ rooms**
Multi-building, operating, ~40 guests. The capacity sweep shows yield rising monotonically with
capacity, and 40 guests is far beyond anything else available. Multi-building structure plausibly
allows **owner accommodation without stopping trade** — the single highest-value attribute
identified in the financial modelling. Hokkaido's snow resilience is the best measured.
*Concern:* Niseko carries ~774 weighted rooms of incoming supply.

**3. Jazz Club Annupuri — ¥130m / A$1.16m — pension, Niseko Annupuri**
The cheapest operating business found, in the strongest snow-resilience region. Note a separate
source describes an Annupuri pension on a **30-year commercial lease within Annupuri Resort** — if
this is the same asset, it is **leasehold, not freehold**, which changes the investment entirely
and must be established first.

### Tier 2 — above the plausible capital range but useful as comparables
Echo Hotel Hakuba (A$6.18m), Ramat Chalet (A$4.00m), QFox Black Diamond (A$6.67m).
Echo Hotel is the single most useful comparable in the project *if* its accounts can be seen: 48
guests in Hakuba is exactly the format the capacity analysis says is economically optimal.

### Tier 3 — out of scope
Shinka Niseko (A$22.2m). Ezomurasaki Furano — **POA and lease-back**, meaning the return is
contractual rather than operational; a different asset class and it does not meet the owners'
"use it personally" requirement.

## 4. Why this route may beat conversion

| Risk | Converting residential | Buying an operating business |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Licence obtainable? | **Unresolved — gating** | **Already held** |
| Sprinkler/fire compliance cost | **¥50k–¥10m unknown** | **Already spent** |
| Renovation cost | ESTIMATE, understated | Known condition, optional |
| Occupancy | **ASSUMPTION — the weakest input** | **Trading history exists** |
| Management available? | Assumed | **Incumbent staff and systems** |
| Time to first revenue | 6–12 months | Immediate |

**Four of the five largest unknowns in this project are resolved by buying something already
operating.** That is worth a substantial premium, and the models do not currently price it.

The offsetting risks are real: businesses for sale are often for sale **because they are
underperforming**, staff and systems may be poor, buildings are typically older, and — as §2
shows — vendor performance claims cannot be taken at face value.
